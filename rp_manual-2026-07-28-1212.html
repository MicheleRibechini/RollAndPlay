Core Rules

Introduction

This is a lightweight, fast Roll and Play system built on shared narration: Keepers tell the story together, leaning on Hooks whenever something needs tracking, and Rolling dice only when they can't agree on what happens next.

At its heart lies a single conflict: the World wants to stay as it is, the Heroes want to change it — even if only in small ways. Every Keeper at the table controls a slice of this struggle: the World Keeper safeguards the status quo, Hero Keepers push against it.

A First Look Example

Two Keepers sit down to play. The World Keeper controls "Drustvar" and "The Black Rain" (Hooks describing the city and the curse hanging over it); the Hero Keeper controls "John Ryan Welsh" (Hook, their detective).

They start narrating together: John walks through Drustvar's rain-soaked streets, and both Keepers agree on the mood, the setting, the small details — no dice needed, they're simply telling a story side by side.

Then John tries to break into a locked warehouse. Here the two Keepers don't agree on the outcome — maybe it opens easily, maybe it doesn't — so they Roll the dice to settle it. The Hero Keeper isn't happy with the result, and spends a resource (a Tarot, a point of Kharma) to nudge it back in John's favor. The World Keeper accepts the trade, and the story moves on.

This is the whole game in miniature: narrate together as long as there's agreement, Roll when there isn't, and spend resources when a Keeper wants to bend the outcome further.

Keepers

Every player at the table is called a Keeper — someone with authority over a portion of the game.

The World Keeper is the custodian of the World, and by that very nature, its arbiter: whoever holds the World also holds the rules that govern it.

Hero Keepers play their Heroes, but their authority doesn't stop there: they also control the Hooks around them in a given moment, narrating whatever serves the scene best, even stepping outside their own Hero if it makes for a better story.

Keepers Example

John's Keeper is narrating a car chase. Rather than just describing John's driving, they also narrate the other driver swerving, a bystander diving out of the way, and a road sign getting clipped — all Hooks around John, not just John himself. If it makes for a better beat, they might even have John's rival make a mistake that isn't strictly "his" to narrate, purely because it serves the scene.

Hooks

A Hook is the basic building block of the story: a character, a location, an object, a feeling, a thought, a memory, an historical event, an action. Heroes are Hooks. So are their antagonists.

Hook Examples
  • John Ryan Welsh (Hero, ~main protagonist)
  • Bolt (object, John's gun)
  • Miranda (pet, John's dog)
  • Drustvar (location, the city-state John lives in)
  • The Black Rain (phenomenon, the black-magic rain over Drustvar)
  • John's Depression (condition)
  • War of Dimistris (historical event)
  • Low Blow (action, wrestling move)

Any Hook can be transcribed as a Wikipedia-style page. We can divide Hooks into categories:

  • Events, describing drive to change
  • Entity, describing forces in motion
  • Trials, describing quick, short term conflicts
  • Quests, describing long term conflicts

A Hook is described by one or more Traits, keywords that define its nature and role in the story.

Traits Examples

Hook: John Ryan Welsh
Traits: detective, Inquisitor, thug, art-lover, alcoholic, smoker, lieutenant, conspiracy-theorist, depressed.

Grades

A Hook carries a Grade showing its value answering the Question: «is this Hook helping the Heroes?». The Grade goes from 1 (absolutely not) to 6 (absolutely yes).

Hook Grade Example

John Ryan Welsh starts at Grade 6/6: he's in his element, doing exactly what his Traits say — investigating, drinking, smoking, appreciating art, cracking conspiracy theories.

As the adventure wears him down — hurt, tired, frustrated — he drops to 2/6. At that point his Traits won't help him much, he'll need to recover energy or fortune.

As a general rule: 1 means completely opposing Heroes (World, status quo, conservation), while 6 means completely helping Heroes (change, revolution).

Readings

Grades are read like runes: Keepers ask a Question and get an Answer that drives the story forward.

Questions must always be phrased so a high Grade favors the Heroes. Not "are these enemies strong?" but "are these enemies weak?".

The main technique is boolean: a yes/no Question, with collateral "ands" and "buts".

Roll 1 2 3 4 5 6
Answer No, and... No. No, but... Yes, but... Yes. Yes, and...
Reading the Hook Grade

John's starting Grade (6/6) answers directly: «yes, he's okay, and furthermore, he's very in shape!». Dropped to 2/6: «no, he's not well at all».

The technique expands into other registers:

Rolls

When a Grade is uncertain, dice are Rolled. Three regular 6-sided dice are thrown, and the median (middle value) is kept. That result becomes the Grade of the Hook, and Keepers must use it to Read.

Roll Example

The World Keeper isn't sure whether a locked door can be forced open, and lets the dice decide: the higher the Roll, the luckier the Heroes are, and the easier the door — and its consequences on other Hooks — will turn out to be.

The Hero Keeper throws: 2, 4, 5. The 4 is the median, becoming the Answer's Grade.

The Reading is: "yes, but…" — the World Keeper narrates it as: «the door gives way, but the noise draws attention from the guards down the hall…».

World Keepers may also Roll the dice for inspiration, but Hero Keepers are the ones actually required to do so.

Narration

The Roll decides who takes control of the narration: on a Success, Hero Keepers describe what happens; on a Failure, the World Keeper does.

Either Keeper can invade the other's controlled Hooks when narrating a Success or a Failure.

Narration Example

John wants to kill a witch. That's an unsure Event, dice are rolled.
Success: John's Keeper describes how he hunts down and kills the witch.
Failure: the witch's Keeper (probably the World Keeper) describes how she eludes him and lures him into the dark woods.

Limits

A Keeper can impose a hard veto when the narration breaks the World's rules. The Keeper narrating still keeps their Success, but must reframe it more plausibly.

Limits Example

John wants to make a gangster boss talk (that's unsure, for sure). His Keeper Rolls a Success, and starts describing: John points a gun at the boss's head to scare him into confessing.

The World Keeper (or whoever controls the Boss Hook) stops them: «it's not plausible that this evil, dark-magic character could ever be scared by something this mundane». John's Keeper reframes: «John takes his Brume Dagger (Hook) instead, since it's effective against magic». «Now we are talking».

Liberties

On a Success, Hero Keepers take control of the narration and can steer the story wherever they want, affecting and transforming multiple Hooks at once.

Liberties Example

John is about to leave on a mission, and Charlie, his protégée, insists on helping. Since it's unsure whether she'll help or not, Keepers propose a Roll, and get a Success: they narrate how, despite John not wanting her involved, Charlie follows him anyway.

The Keeper used a Success to narrate something against their own character's interests, because it made for a more interesting story. No one vetoes it, and the narration stands.

Extentions

Events

An Event is a Hook describing a single change, proposed by a Keeper. If no one objects, it happens as described; if someone Questions it, dice are Rolled to decide who narrates the outcome.

Event Example

John's Keeper starts narrating Events. Until someone puts a veto on them, John's Keeper can continue narrating the Event (both the action itself and its consequences).

At a certain point, John's Keeper crosses the limit: «John punches Isaiah, Rootshield's family scion, in front of his bodyguards», but the World Keeper Questions it.

Dice are Rolled: 4/6, yes, but. John's Keeper narrates the hit landing, but the World Keeper adds the twist: the bodyguards notice, and they're already moving in.

An Event can be fixed into an Entity: when a Roll's outcome is useful enough to keep around as a tactical resource — an ally, a tool, an obstacle, a threat — the World Keeper can lock it in as an Entity, carrying over the very Grade rolled for the Event.

Event to Entity Example

John's Keeper proposes an Event: John buys a round for the bouncer at a shady club, trying to get on his good side. Dice are Rolled: 4/6, yes but. Rather than let the moment simply pass, the World Keeper turns it into an Entity: "Bouncer Contact" at Grade 4/6 — a favor John can call on later, whenever getting into that club matters again.

The same logic works the other way: if John had barricaded a door instead, a bad Roll might just as easily become a "Flimsy Barricade" Entity at 2/6 — a real but shaky obstacle, ready to give the World Keeper leverage down the line.

It can also be challenged into a Trial, turning a Event's outcome into a Standing that future Rolls will build on.

Event to Trial Example

John's Keeper proposes an Event: John investigates the crime scene for clues. Dice are Rolled: 3/6. John's Keeper isn't satisfied — there has to be more here — so instead of settling for it, the World Keeper spins it into a Trial: "Cracking the Case" starts at Grade 3/6, to be Read over several Rolls as John keeps digging rather than closing the matter with one throw of the dice.

Finally, an Event with high enough stakes can be promoted directly into a Quest, turning a single success into the first step of a much longer pursuit.

Finally, an Event with high enough stakes can be promoted directly into a Quest, turning a single success into the first step of a much longer pursuit.

Event to Quest Example

John's Keeper proposes an Event: John tries to catch the eye of Mary Garden, Drustvar's muse. Dice are Rolled: 5/6, a clear Success. The stakes are too big for a single Event to settle, though, so the World Keeper turns it into a Quest instead: "Winning Mary's Heart," starting at Grade 3/6 instead of 2/6 — the Success counts, but only as the first step of a pursuit that will take much longer to actually reach 5/6.

Entities

Entities are Hooks that represent the status of a force in play, pursuing a specific Goal or purpose. Because they track a force in motion, their Grade tends to swing quickly and drastically, rising or falling as that force gains or loses ground.

Entity Example

John visits his burly alchemist friend Viktor. When he arrives, the World Keeper makes the "Viktor Lubnik" Entity available at Grade 6/6 — his goodwill and resources ready to be tapped. Once the Heroes leave, the Entity Hook is no longer available.

Entities are physically represented at the table with an Entity Card: a small piece of paper with the Entity's name and its Traits written on it, and a die placed on top showing its current Grade.

Entity Card Example

John gets dragged into a bar brawl. The World Keeper quickly sets up a handful of Entity Cards to track everything that matters: "Thug Marek" at Grade 2/6 and "Thug Otto" at Grade 3/6 (both hostile — low Grade means they're still standing and dangerous), "Rickety Table" at Grade 2/6 (just an obstacle John can shove people into, not a real threat either way), and "Old Friend Sam" at Grade 2/6, an ally caught in the fray who's currently too rattled to be of any real help.

As the brawl goes on, Marek takes a solid hit and rises to 5/6 — knocked down and out of the fight — while Sam, cornered and forced to actually swing a bottle, also rises to 5/6, but for him that means he's finally pulling real weight. Every Keeper at the table can glance at the cards and immediately read who's winning, who's still a threat, and who's stepping up.

Entity Hooks work with Kharma, and when directly threatened or challenged in a conflict, they can temporarily become Trials.

Trials

A Trial is a temporary Hook that describes a conflict, lasting only minutes of gameplay, though it can grow into a longer-lasting Quest if needed.

Trial rules:

  • World Keeper decides the starting Grade of the Trial based on the Question raised
  • When an Event Succeeds against a Trial, its Grade increases by one
  • When an Event Fails against a Trial, its Grade decreases by one
Trial Example

John is racing at the Grand Prix. The Question: «is he winning?» Answer: «no» 2/6. Since John really wants to win, a Trial begins. On the first Event, John Fails: his car stalls while everyone else pulls ahead; the Trial drops to 1/6: «he's not winning, and, in fact, he's last».

But his Keeper isn't ready to give up, and the World Keeper agrees — the race has barely started, there's still plenty that could happen. The Trial continues…

Keepers must describe how the Event makes the Trial progress toward either the good or the bad ending.

Trial Progress Example

John is at 3/6. Another Success brings him to 4/6, "yes, but" territory — he's first, even briefly.

His Keeper describes: «John pushes on» and the World Keeper stops them: «you're reaching the front, you're in pole position, describe how!». «I had two cars ahead, right? I see a curve and cut the corner, reaching the top».

A Trial ends when the World Keeper calls Timing on it.

Quests

A Quest is essentially a long-term Trial that plays out over sessions instead of minutes, following the same rules but at a slower pace, progressing once per in-game month, season, session, or chapter at the World Keeper's discretion.

A Quest's closure ripples into World Hooks and Heroes' Traits — its effects don't stay contained to the Quest itself, they reshape the World and the Hero around it. When the Quest ends is a matter of Timing.

Quest Example

John discovers his peculiar bio-chemical nature and wants to find out more. The World Keeper creates the "Son of Brume" Quest, which will plausibly make him a stronger inquisitor and may grant new Traits or Hooks.

The World Keeper would normally start it at Grade 2, but since John already did research on the topic, they start at 3/6: «no, but…».

Standings

A Hook's Grade can act as a Standing: a default answer on a given Question that applies without needing a Roll, taking its place until an Event actively challenges it.

Standing Example

John's "Relationship with the Great Families" Quest Hook sits at 2/6. Even without a Roll, this is the default: doors are shut, and the Great Families' people look at him with suspicion or open contempt. This holds until someone challenges it.

If a Keeper wants an Event that pushes against a Standing for a specific, momentary purpose, without aiming to actually change the Standing itself, a Trial begins at a Grade equal to the Standing. The Standing itself stays untouched unless the story, through a Quest or a Bonfire, actually moves it.

Traits

A Hook is defined by Traits: keywords or short descriptions that tell us what it is, what it's made of, what it can be used for (see Kharma) and what it shouldn't be used for (see Limits). A Hook should have a Trait at least.

Traits Examples

Hook: John Ryan Welsh
Traits: detective, Inquisitor, thug, art-lover, alcoholic, smoker, lieutenant, conspiracy-theorist, depressed.

Some Traits are self-explanatory, "stealthy" just means stealthy, while others are tied to the World's own lore. These World-bound Traits should come with a short description, so every Keeper shares the same understanding of what they mean

World-bound Trait Description Example

Trait: Inquisitor
Description: Member of Drustvar's army, specialized in neutralizing magic and hunting witches and abominations, thanks to a high concentration of Brume in their body. Inquisitors are in fact created as children, exposed to high concentrations of radioactive Brume to alter their genetics.

Some Traits remain implicit: we can infer John is a male human physical-approach occult investigator even though none of this is ever stated outright. Keepers usually describe their Hooks, especially Heroes, with a more detailed background, since a longer description makes implicit Traits easier to surface.

Promotions

Traits can be promoted into Hooks, becoming quantified by a Grade: the moment the story needs to ask a Question about it and track its answer over time, it stops being a mere descriptor and becomes a Hook of its own.

Trait to Hook Example

John carries the Trait "wounded" after a Trial. As long as no one asks how bad the wound is, it stays a simple Trait — true or not, nothing more. But the moment someone raises the Question «how well is he recovering?», it needs an Answer with a Grade: the Trait becomes a Hook, and — if the wound matters enough to the story — that Hook might grow into its own Trial or even Quest, tracking his recovery Event by Event.

Eventualities

Keepers can spend their "ands" and "buts" more mechanically through Chance and Setback. A Chance is a small positive Event that helps the Heroes; a Setback is a small negative Event that hinders them.

Roll 1 2 3 4 5 6
Answer No, and... No. No, but... Yes, but... Yes. Yes, and...
Eventualities Failure + Setback Failure Failure + Chance Success + Setback Success Success + Chance
  • Chance: create a new Hook at Grade 4/6, or increase an existing Grade by 1
  • Setback: create a new Hook at Grade 3/6, or decrease an existing Grade by 1

The World Keeper has full control over Chances and especially Setbacks, but should coordinate with Hero Keepers to create something interesting for everyone.

These new Hooks start as temporary Entity or Trial Hooks, but can become Quests if the narrative calls for it.

Eventualities to Quest Example

John tries to talk his way past a checkpoint guarding the road to the capital. He Rolls a 4: yes, but. The World Keeper interprets it via Eventualities: a Success (the guards let him through) paired with a Setback (something else is revealed).

The World Keeper creates a "Guards Suspicious of John" Entity at Grade 3/6 (the Setback). Over the following Events, this Entity keeps weighing on John's actions — the guards flag his name, question his companions, slow him down at other checkpoints.

Eventually John's Keeper decides to deal with it directly, challenging the Entity into a Trial: he tries to clear his name and shake off their attention. He Reads it over a few Rolls, but never manages to push Suspicion below 3/6 — the guards remain wary no matter what he does.

John ultimately leaves the region without resolving it. Seeing the thread has more weight than a closed Trial deserves, the World Keeper turns it into a Quest instead — the guards report him up the chain, and it's only a matter of time before that attention catches up with him somewhere down the line.

Kharma

During a Roll, a Keeper can swap the Grade of a controlled, Trait-coherent Entity with the current median die.

Kharma Example

John is at Grade 5/6. During a Roll involving his Detective skills (Trait), dice are 2, 2, 4. It's a bad Roll, but John's Keeper knows this is exactly his territory, so they swap John's 5/6 for the median, 2/6. The World Keeper agrees: John drops to 2/6, the Roll rises to 2, 4, 5 (median 4/6).

Instead of a full swap, Keepers can go step by step: reducing one Grade to increase a lower Grade, or vice versa. You cannot reduce a Grade to raise a higher one, or vice versa — the idea is bringing two values into balance, not feeding one from the other.

Kharma Small Steps Example

John is at Grade 5/6. The following Roll is 2, 3, 5. Wanting a Success, and with John's Grade higher than the median, the Keeper spends one point of John's Grade (down to 4) to raise the median to 4. New Roll: 2, 4, 5, John at 4/6.

The Keeper would like to spend another point to push it to 5, but can't: the Grade must still be higher than the die to feed it a point, and now they're equal.

Kharma can only be used from Entities with Traits extremely coherent with the current Roll.

Kharma Traits Example

Charlie's Keeper wants to use Kharma with her. Charlie is John's co-pilot, with the Trait Spotter, so they explain: «Charlie is used to quickly relaying positions and rapid actions to comrades. She does the same here». The World Keeper allows it.

At the table, keep each Entity's Kharma die sitting on top of its card. When that Entity is spent for Kharma in a Roll, nudge the die slightly off-center on the card — a visible flag that it's already been used and can't be swapped or stepped again until the Roll ends.

Kharma Limit Example

John's Keeper swaps his Grade with the median, turning a 2 into a 5. Later in the same Roll, the World Keeper tries to undo it by swapping John's Grade again, pulling the median back down — but John's die has already been nudged this Roll, and the swap is refused, preventing endless back-and-forth.

Tarots

Six double-sided cards called Tarots represent how fate influences the Roll. All Keepers share the same deck and can use it on any Roll — even, especially, other players'.

Each Tarot rerolls a specific die and triggers a secondary effect on Success or Failure, raising or lowering the median. Cards split into two groups: front ◆ (positive) and back ◇ (negative).

Tarot names, tags and readings should be used as Traits to interpret how fate influences the Roll. When used, a Tarot rotates horizontally, marking it "in use" (can't be used again on this Roll). Once resolved, it rotates back vertical and flips to the other side, changing nature.

Tarot Example

John's hunting a monster. His Keeper rolls badly (1, 2, 2) and turns to Tarots. Since hunting monsters is second nature to John, they use The Expertise: reroll the lowest die, +1 on Failure.

They rotate the card horizontally, reroll the 1 into a 3. New Roll: 2, 2, 3. Resolving: the Event Fails, so the median gains 1 from The Expertise, becoming 3, total: 2, 3, 3. Better than nothing.
They rotate the card back and flip it: it becomes The Pride.

Multiple Tarots can be used on a single Roll: either Hero Keepers and the World Keeper can decide to use Tarots to reroll dice. Any moment, Keepers can decide to apply the effect of a Tarot in use, if triggered: the World Keeper can apply the effect of only ◇ Tarots, while Hero Keepers can apply the effect of only ◆ Tarots. The World Keeper always have the priority to apply their ◇ Tarots first.

Resolution Example

During an investigation, John's Keeper draws on his profession: The Talent ⇓✓◆ and The Expertise ⇓✕◆. The World Keeper, unconvinced the case will go John's way, draws The Trauma ⇑✕◇ against him. Current roll: 2, 3, 5.

The World Keeper resolves first: The Trauma ✕◇ triggers on Failure, and the Event is currently Failing on a 3 — the median drops by 1, becoming 2. The card rotates from horizontal back to vertical and flips to The Growth ✓◆, ready to be used again. New Roll: 2, 2, 5.

Now the Hero Keeper resolves: The Expertise ✕◆ also triggers on Failure — the median gains 1, becoming 3. New Roll: 2, 3, 5. The Talent ✓◆ triggers on Success, but the Event is still Failing, so it doesn't apply — it stays horizontal, unresolved, until its trigger becomes relevant or the Event ends.

John's Keeper draws The Duty ⇔✕◆ to press on. The World Keeper draws The Aberration ⇑✓◇ in response.

The Aberration triggers on Success, and the Event is still Failing, so it doesn't apply yet. The Duty ✕◆ triggers on Failure — the median gains 1, becoming 4. New Roll: 2, 4, 5. The Event now Succeeds, so The Talent ✓◆ finally triggers: the median gains 1, becoming 5.

The World Keeper resolves The Aberration ✓◇: the Event is Succeeding, so it applies — the median drops by 1, becoming 4. Final Roll: 2, 4, 5. Final Grade: 4.

Invocations

A Keeper can directly trigger a Tarot's effect without rolling it — an Invocation. They flip the card to its back and add (◆) or remove (◇) 1 from the median, depending on the Tarot invoked. This is both a way to influence the Roll and reorganize the cards.

Invocation Example

A Keeper rolls 3, 4, 5, not enough. They invoke The Ambition, flipping it to its back (The Aberration) and gaining +1 on the median. Final Roll: 3, 5, 5.

Combos

Keepers can freely mix Tarots and Kharma, narrating the details of how the Event unfolds as resources are spent. This fuels the narrative, inspiring other Keepers to reach for more Tarots and Kharma in turn.

Timing

Timing is the World Keeper's authority over the World's space and time, and over the rhythm of the narration itself — including knowing when an arc has run its course, whether that's a single scene, an action, a dialogue, or an entire saga.

In practice, this often means fixing a Hook's Grade in place at whatever value it currently holds, stopping it from progressing any further.

Timing an Event Example

John is trying to pick a lock. His Keeper keeps drawing Tarots and spending Kharma, chasing a better Roll — five minutes pass, the table stalls on this single die roll. The World Keeper calls Timing: "Enough — it went this way, unfortunately. You'll get your chance to make up for it later." The lock stays shut, and the story moves on.

Timing a Trial Example

John is dueling a rival swordsman. The Trial climbed to 4/6 at its peak, but a string of disastrous Events drags it down to 2/6. The World Keeper calls Timing right there: "This is the moment — he's disarmed, humiliated in front of the crowd." The swing from a real high point to a real low point is exactly what makes it the right beat to end on.

Timing a Quest Example

John has spent several sessions investigating the smuggling ring behind the docks, the Quest sitting at 5/6. The case gets formally closed — not every thread is tied up, but enough is: names are named, the ring is broken. The World Keeper calls Timing: "The investigation closes here, at 5/6 — you didn't get everything, but you got enough to call it a win." The narrative event (the case closing) justifies freezing the Grade just short of a perfect outcome.

This is what actually tips the balance of authority toward the World Keeper. Used well, it keeps the pace of the game sharp. Used badly — say, always calling Timing right when a Grade dips against the Heroes, never when it rises — it turns into a cheap way to grind the story down without opposition, and kills everyone's fun at the table.

The World Keeper should wield Timing in service of a good story for the whole table, not as a leash on the Heroes.

Update

Always remember to update the Roll's median every time the result is influenced.

Update Example

John's Roll is 2, 5, 4. The Keeper orders them: 2, 4, 5. Median: 4. A Tarot rerolls the 2 into a 6: new Roll 4, 5, 6. Kharma swaps a die for a Grade: the 5 becomes 6, final Roll 4, 6, 6. Result: 6!

Bonfires

At any moment, the World Keeper can decide to reorganize all game resources — Hooks, Tarots, Grades — with a Bonfire. It typically happens at the end of a Quest, after a high-intensity Trial, during a time skip, or whenever Heroes and World take a narrative breath.

A Bonfire works as follows:

  • Tarots reset to their front ◆.
  • Kharma resets to 6/6 for Hero-controlled Hooks and 1/6 for World-controlled Hooks
  • If a Kharma Hook is linked to a Quest, its resets to the Quest's Grade.

Hero Keepers can ask for a Bonfire, but the World Keeper can always refuse if it isn't interesting for the story.

Every Bonfire automatically generates a Setback that the World Keeper can trigger at any point during the session — a piece of dynamite ready to go off whenever needed. If unused by the end of the session, it's gone: it doesn't stack, it doesn't carry over.

Bonfire Example

John and his friends decide to take a break, a Bonfire is triggered. Tarots resets by flipping on their front, hostile Hooks reset to 1/6, friendly Hooks reset to 6/6. Since John has a "Son of Brume" quest at 4/6, his Grade starts at 4/6 instead of 6/6.

World Keeper gets a Setback, which they decide to spend to create a new 3/6 Hook.

A Bonfire is also the most natural moment to advance Quests in bulk, reflecting the narrative time that has passed. Unlike Trials, Quest advancement at a Bonfire isn't bound to the ±1 step — it can jump directly to a higher or lower value if the narrative justifies it.

Bonfire Quest Update Example

During a Bonfire marking the shift from autumn to winter, the World Keeper decides the plague afflicting the region has run its course. The "Survive the Plague" Quest, stuck at 4/6, jumps straight to 6/6: the passing of time resolved what Events alone hadn't yet closed.

Discord

Heroes are meant to act as a united force against the World, but moments of Discord can still arise — between Hero Keepers off-game, or between Heroes in-fiction. When this happens, the priority should always be talking it out and finding concordance: this system isn't built for Hero-versus-Hero conflict, and its mechanics don't arbitrate who "wins" between Heroes.

Only in cases of real, unresolved Discord should Keepers fall back on the dice. One Hero Keeper can take initiative and propose the Event, rolling as usual, while the other spends resources (Kharma, Tarots) trying to make it Fail. It works, but it should stay the exception, not the norm.

Starting

Every Hero is, like all Hooks, defined by a name and Traits.

The World Keeper can provide a list of Traits tailored to their World, balanced by narrative weight — "inquisitor" carries the same weight as "alchemist", no Trait is objectively stronger than another.

Every Hero is created by choosing:

  • 3 positive Traits
  • 2 negative Traits
  • 2 neutral Traits (defining the Hero's nature without being a strength or a flaw)

A Hero Keeper can inflict additional negative Traits on themselves: for each one, they gain a new positive Trait and a new negative Trait in return. Final balance always stays a negotiation with the World Keeper.

A Hero's starting Grade is always 6/6, like every Hero-controlled Hook: they start in their element, before the World begins wearing them down.

Minor Hooks — NPCs, side characters, relevant objects — follow the same logic at a smaller scale: 2 or 3 Traits (positive, negative, neutral) are enough, without the full 3+2+2 structure reserved for Heroes.

Starting Example

The World Keeper proposes for Drustvar: detective, alchemist, smuggler, fallen noble, vengeful spirit, addict, seer, corrupt.
John's player picks: detective, lieutenant, art-lover (positive), alcoholic, conspiracy-theorist (negative), smoker, inquisitor (neutral). John starts at Grade 6/6.

Growth

Heroes gain and lose Traits constantly, depending on how they were earned:

  • From an Event: an ephemeral Trait, lasting a scene or so (e.g. "stealthy")
  • From a Trial: a medium-duration Trait, which may spawn further Trials (e.g. "wounded")
  • From a Quest: a permanent Trait, because a Quest changes the Hero's nature — it makes them discover themselves. Completing it can grant a new positive Trait, or remove a negative one: balance stays with the World Keeper

Ephemeral and Trial-born Traits fade on their own once their narrative window closes — only Quest-born Traits truly accumulate, and there's no cap on those.

Chains

Traits can be linked in Chains: Trait B can only be gained if Trait A is already held. This creates a strong specialization dynamic, rewarding Heroes who commit to a direction instead of spreading thin.

Chains don't have to be linear. A Trait can branch into several possible follow-ups, or require more than one prerequisite Trait at once, forming something closer to a skill tree than a straight line — and letting players pursue their own "build" over the course of the story.

Chain Example

John holds inquisitor. Completing the "Son of Brume" Quest, his Keeper could choose between exorcist (requires only inquisitor) or brume-touched (requires both inquisitor and conspiracy-theorist), each opening different follow-up Traits down the line — exorcist leads toward bane-marked, while brume-touched leads toward hollow-blooded.

The World Keeper is the one who designs these Chains for their World, deciding which Traits gate which — the same way they curate the initial Trait list at Starting.

Chains can go beyond simple prerequisites and rely on thresholds instead: a Trait becomes available not because one specific Trait is already held, but because the Hook holds enough Traits of a certain kind to justify it. As always, the World Keeper decides both the threshold-locked Trait and the pool of Traits that count toward it.

Threshold-Chain Example

Trait: Athlete
Description: This Hook is athletic enough to overcome any mundane physical challenge with ease.
Requirement: 3 or more Traits from the following physical pool — Inquisitor, Bodybuilder, Gymnast, Brawler, Duelist, Survivor, Brume-Touched.
